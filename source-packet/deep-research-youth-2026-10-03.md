# Evidence Gaps and Pilot Design for a Youth Rap-and-Learning Website

## Executive synthesis

The strongest conclusion from the available evidence is **not** that a rap-themed website will make teenagers value school. It is narrower and more useful: when learners themselves generate connections between something they care about and an educational skill, brief relevance interventions can sometimes increase interest, performance, course-taking, or persistence. By contrast, simply telling people why something is useful can be ineffective and, for people with low confidence, can sometimes reduce interest or performance. Canning and Harackiewicz's three-study laboratory program is especially important here: self-generated utility performed better than imposed utility for low-confidence participants, while combining supplied examples with opportunities for self-generation could mitigate the downside of direct messaging. fileciteturn2file0L1-L1

There is also a major **interest-to-action gap**. The closest study I found to a voluntary online educational behavior among high-school students randomized 269 students to utility-value, implementation-intention, combined, or control conditions. Neither minimal intervention increased actual participation in the voluntary training. Students' initial expectancy for success and grades predicted participation instead. fileciteturn4file0L1-L1 That null result should weigh heavily in the website's theory of change: making a skill *sound relevant* is not the same as getting a teenager to *do something educational with it*.

Meanwhile, randomized evidence does show that relevance interventions can affect behavior under some circumstances. In one high-school science experiment, 262 students were randomized within classes to write about how science connected to their lives or to summarize material; the relevance exercise increased interest and grades primarily among students with low expectations of success. citeturn4search0 In another intervention, parents received two brochures plus a website designed to help them discuss the usefulness of mathematics and science; students in intervention families subsequently took about one additional semester of mathematics/science during the final two years of high school. But because the treatment was a **three-part parent intervention**, that experiment does not identify the causal effect of the website alone. citeturn9search1turn9search17

The evidence is therefore sufficient to justify a **small, falsifiable formative pilot**, but not a claim that the proposed website will reconnect rap-ambitious youth to school. The most defensible pilot question is:

> **Can a rap-production-centered, autonomy-supportive website help adolescents identify and voluntarily take one concrete learning action that they themselves see as useful to their music goals, without increasing reactance, perceived disrespect, school rejection, or pressure to pursue college?**

That framing also fixes a problem with the original premise. The juvenile-defender-office observation is an anecdote and must remain an anecdote; it is not evidence that a substantial percentage of U.S. adolescents, Black youth, justice-involved youth, or aspiring rappers believe school is unnecessary. The closest qualitative aspiration study located here interviewed 29 young Black men involved in rap production and found that their aspirations changed with feedback, production opportunities, finances, and broader social/economic circumstances—useful for understanding complexity, but incapable of estimating prevalence or establishing that rap ambition causes school disengagement. citeturn0search9

**The design should consequently affirm the ambition rather than challenge it.** A productive message architecture is not “rap is unlikely, so stay in school.” It is closer to: **“You have a music goal. Professional creative work uses many skills. Which ones would help *your* next project? Pick one you want to build.”** School can appear as one possible source of skills and opportunities alongside libraries, community programs, collaborators, tutorials, practice, work experience, vocational education, certification, apprenticeships, and postsecondary education. Nothing in the evidence warrants presenting a four-year degree as mandatory for legitimate artistic ambition.

## What the original studies actually show

### Self-generated utility is substantially better grounded than persuasion

Canning and Harackiewicz's 2015 paper, *Teach It, Don't Preach It*, directly addresses one of the most important design choices for this project. Across three laboratory studies using a novel mental-math learning task, participants either generated their own utility connections or encountered experimenter-provided utility information. In the first study, directly communicated utility information undermined performance and interest among people lacking confidence, whereas self-generated utility had positive effects. A second study found that the negative effect could be ameliorated when participants were also invited to generate their own examples; a third suggested that everyday-life examples worked better than school/career examples for low-confidence participants. fileciteturn2file0L1-L1

**Transfer limit:** these were controlled mental-math studies, not U.S. adolescents considering school, not aspiring musicians, and not voluntary website users. The study establishes a message-design mechanism, not efficacy for this proposed product. The publicly retrieved abstract also does not report the individual study sample sizes, so I would not invent those figures.

The website implication is nevertheless unusually clear: **do not construct the experience around adult-written arguments about why algebra, English, history, science, or school “really matter for rap.”** Let the user start with a goal and produce the connection.

For example, a module might ask:

> “What are you trying to get better at right now?”

A user could choose or write “writing,” “beats,” “recording,” “performing,” “releasing music,” “making money from music,” “working with people,” or something else. Only after that selection should the system surface relevant capabilities and let the learner decide which connection is useful. This preserves self-persuasion rather than disguising an institutional message as youth culture.

### Relevance can change school outcomes, but transfer is highly conditional

Hulleman and Harackiewicz's 2009 high-school field experiment is one of the more relevant causal studies because it involves adolescents rather than college students. The study included 262 students taught by seven teachers in 19 classrooms at two Midwestern high schools, with students randomized within classrooms to a relevance-writing or control-summary condition. Most were ninth graders. Students in the intervention repeatedly explained how course material could be useful in their own lives. Effects on interest and grades were concentrated among students with lower expectations for success. citeturn4search0

That is encouraging for a “find your own connection” design, but the transfer distance remains large. Students were already enrolled in science, teachers administered the exercise, writing was embedded in classroom work, and the principal downstream “future” measure concerned interest in science courses/careers rather than observed subsequent enrollment. citeturn4search0

A more behaviorally meaningful high-school result comes from Harackiewicz and colleagues' parent intervention. Families were assigned to treatment or control; intervention parents received two brochures plus the *Choices Ahead* website explaining mathematics/science utility and encouraging parent–teen conversations. The final transcript sample contained 181 adolescents, and intervention students averaged 8.31 semesters of mathematics/science in their final two high-school years versus 7.50 in the control condition. citeturn9search1turn9search17

That is a genuine randomized difference in later course-taking, but it **cannot be attributed to the website**. The causal treatment was the package—printed materials, website, and parent communication. The original authors also treated some proposed mechanisms cautiously because the relevant parent/student measures were not temporally arranged for a clean mediation test. The correct inference is “a multifaceted utility-value intervention directed through parents affected later STEM course-taking,” not “educational websites change adolescent course-taking.” citeturn9search1

College studies strengthen the general mechanism while simultaneously illustrating the transfer problem. Canning and colleagues randomized 577 introductory-biology students across repeated utility-value versus summary assignments. Any exposure to the relevance-writing condition was associated experimentally with better course grades, higher subsequent biology enrollment, and lower probability of abandoning a STEM major; timing interacted with prior performance. fileciteturn8file0L1-L1 Asher and colleagues later randomized 2,505 gateway-chemistry students to three personal-relevance essays or control essays and observed 74% versus 70% STEM-major persistence 2.5 years later, with a larger treatment contrast among marginalized and underrepresented racial/ethnic students. fileciteturn7file0L1-L1

Those are substantially stronger endpoints than “students liked the lesson.” But they concern students **already enrolled in college STEM courses**, completing assigned coursework inside existing educational structures. They therefore demonstrate the potential of self-generated relevance, not that an optional website can convert a skeptical adolescent's musical ambition into educational action.

Most importantly, van der Beek and Hertel provide a close counterexample. Their randomized study assigned 269 high-school students to utility-value, implementation-intention, combined, or control conditions before optional online self-regulated-learning training. The interventions did **not** increase participation. fileciteturn4file0L1-L1 A theory of change for this site must be able to explain—and survive—the possibility that adolescents agree that a skill is useful while doing nothing further.

### Adolescent relevance interventions can also create costs

Gaspard and colleagues' 2015 cluster-randomized study involved 1,916 ninth graders in 82 mathematics classrooms assigned to two relevance treatments or a waiting control. The intervention lasted 90 minutes and combined a presentation with either relevance-writing or evaluation of interview quotations. Six weeks and five months later, both treatments produced more favorable value beliefs; the quotation condition affected a broader set of values than the writing condition. fileciteturn5file0L1-L1

But a later scale-up should prevent overly optimistic interpretation. In a cluster-randomized trial involving 1,744 ninth graders in 78 mathematics classrooms, Gaspard and colleagues again found higher utility value after the intervention—but **also higher perceived cost** in both intervention conditions. Achievement effects were not uniformly present across implementers. fileciteturn6file0L1-L1

That finding matters greatly for this project. A teenager might leave the website thinking:

> “Okay, I see how much work all of this takes—and now rap feels harder and school feels harder.”

An intervention could therefore increase perceived usefulness while simultaneously making the desired path feel more burdensome. **“Utility increased” cannot be the sole success criterion.**

## Hip-hop literacy, culture, autonomy, and language

The hip-hop education literature offers a different kind of evidence: it gives strong reasons to design respectfully and substantive examples of what authentic hip-hop-centered learning can look like, but much of it cannot establish causal effects on educational behavior.

Jabari Evans's 2019 *Deeper than Rap* study is especially useful because the primary paper is publicly accessible. The program was a roughly 10-week school-based hip-hop music program involving writing, production, recording, mixing, and collaborative studio work. The study involved 30 African American sixth- and eighth-grade students—15 girls and 15 boys—selected through a purposive process involving student interest/nominations rather than random assignment. The researcher observed classes, collected field notes, interviewed teaching artists, and analyzed program artifacts. citeturn19view0

The paper's own limitations are important. Participation was self-selected rather than randomized; youth already interested in hip-hop or production may have been especially likely to engage; characteristics of the teaching artist could have contributed to engagement; and the findings were not presented as generally representative. citeturn20view1 The paper also raises a particularly relevant caution: hip-hop artistic practice may have educational value **as artistic practice itself**, not merely as a hook used to smuggle conventional school material into a lesson. citeturn20view1

That suggests a hard design rule:

**Rap should be the subject and practice, not the bait.**

Teaching compression by actually comparing audio dynamics, writing through revision of one's own verse structure, budgeting a release, evaluating a contract scenario, or understanding sampling/licensing questions is more culturally credible than changing “train A leaves the station” into “rapper A leaves the studio” and calling that culturally relevant.

There is one bibliographic discrepancy worth making explicit. The prompt describes “Evans 2015” as qualitative with **31 respondents and no comparison**. I could not substantiate that exact record in the accessible public corpus. The identifiable primary Jabari Evans study I could verify is **2019 and reports 30 student participants**, not 31. citeturn19view0 The 2015/31 item should therefore remain unresolved rather than being silently merged with the 2019 paper.

Lauren Leigh Kelly's work similarly supports the relevance of critical hip-hop literacy, but the public record I located does not expose enough of the full methods to justify pretending that all design details are known. The abstract describes a semester-long teacher-researcher case study in a high-school Hip Hop Literature and Culture course concerned with youth identities, critical literacy, and self-actualization. citeturn17search10 Because full methods are gated in the accessible version, the correct evidence grade here is **qualitative/case evidence with incomplete publicly verifiable methods**, not an efficacy trial.

Morrell and Duncan-Andrade's influential classroom work likewise provides a pedagogical example rather than a controlled effect estimate: students connected hip-hop texts with academic literacy practices and produced extended critical writing. It is valuable as design precedent but should not be cited as proving that hip-hop instruction raises academic attainment. citeturn17search7turn17search4

### Language varieties should be treated as resources

Django Paris's culturally sustaining pedagogy framework explicitly argues that education should sustain rather than eradicate linguistic, literate, and cultural pluralism. fileciteturn10file0L1-L1 H. Samy Alim's critical hip-hop language pedagogy likewise treats language varieties and the power relations surrounding them as objects for critical inquiry rather than deficiencies to be corrected. fileciteturn11file0L1-L1

That has concrete product implications. The site should not:

- automatically convert African American English or other youth/community varieties into standardized academic English and label the latter “correct”;
- equate professionalism with erasing dialect;
- treat slang, code-switching, code-meshing, rhyme-driven syntax, or vernacular spelling as evidence of deficient literacy.

Instead, it can expand repertoire: “Here is how this phrase works in your artistic voice; here is how the same idea might be phrased in a contract email, grant application, press bio, or school assignment.” That communicates **situational choice and audience awareness**, not linguistic hierarchy. This recommendation follows directly from the pluralist orientation of culturally sustaining and critical hip-hop language pedagogies. fileciteturn10file0L1-L1 fileciteturn11file0L1-L1

### Autonomy should be treated as an outcome, not decorative tone

A six-week diary study of 208 high-school students in 41 science classes found that perceived choice, attention to student interests, rationales, and opportunities to ask questions tracked changes in autonomous motivation and engagement, whereas controlling messages, suppression of students' perspectives, and uninteresting activities tracked controlled motivation and disaffection. Because this was a repeated observational diary design rather than randomized manipulation, these relationships should not be described as causal. fileciteturn13file0L1-L1

The broader reactance mechanism is also relevant: psychological reactance concerns motivation to restore freedom when freedom is perceived as threatened. Adolescent observational work has found meaningful variation in reactance and behavior, although such evidence does not establish that this particular website wording will cause disengagement. citeturn15view1turn15view2

Accordingly, seemingly friendly copy such as:

> “You need school even if you want to rap.”

is conceptually wrong for the intervention. So are softer versions such as:

> “Real rappers know education matters.”

Both prescribe the conclusion and threaten identity ownership.

Better:

> “What are you trying to make happen with your music?”

> “Which of these skills would make your next track, performance, release, or business move stronger?”

> “Where would *you* want to learn that skill?”

Then school-related resources can be options rather than the hidden predetermined answer.

## A revised website theory of change and pilot

The original theory of change should be narrowed from something like:

**rap ambition → show educational importance → value school → stay in school**

to:

**existing music goal → authentic artistic task → self-generated skill relevance → learner-selected next action → observable voluntary action**, while monitoring autonomy, reactance, perceived cost, cultural credibility, and school attitudes for deterioration.

This is much closer to what the evidence can justify. fileciteturn2file0L1-L1 fileciteturn4file0L1-L1

### Prototype structure

A low-risk prototype could contain three short routes:

| Route | User experience | Research purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Make something** | Choose an artistic task: revise writing, arrange a track, record clean audio, plan a release, prepare a performance | Tests whether authentic music practice itself sustains engagement |
| **Build a skill** | User chooses which capability matters to *their* goal; site offers multiple learning routes | Tests self-generated utility and autonomy |
| **See how artists work** | Short, sourced process accounts showing drafting, revision, practice, collaboration, business learning, experimentation, setbacks | Tests whether process stories increase realistic strategy knowledge without discouraging ambition |

Crucially, the final step should be **choice of action**, not agreement with a message. Examples include saving a skill plan, completing a short exercise, opening/download­ing a free learning resource, choosing a relevant high-school course to investigate, identifying a teacher/community resource they might choose to ask later, or creating a self-directed practice goal. There should always be non-school routes so that selecting “school” is meaningful rather than forced.

Because the requested research phase explicitly excludes recruitment and data collection, these are **pilot specifications**, not instructions to begin contacting adolescents.

### Primary pilot outcomes

The pilot should predefine a small number of outcomes that separate persuasion from action.

**Primary behavioral outcome: voluntary skill action.** Define before testing what counts: for example, completing an optional skill activity after the relevance prompt rather than merely clicking it. This directly addresses the failure mode seen in the 269-student voluntary-online-training RCT, where utility messaging did not increase participation. fileciteturn4file0L1-L1

**Primary psychological outcome: self-generated utility.** Ask the learner to state, in their own words, whether and how a selected skill could help a personally chosen music goal. Score whether a genuine connection is produced rather than whether the user agrees with “school is useful.” This is much more faithful to the intervention mechanism tested by Canning and Harackiewicz. fileciteturn2file0L1-L1

**Co-primary safety outcome: autonomy/reactance.** Items should assess perceived choice and pressure separately—for example, whether the site felt like it respected the user's decision-making, whether it seemed to have a hidden agenda, whether it was “trying to talk me out of music,” and whether it made the user want to reject the message. High utility coupled with high reactance should not be scored as success. The distinction is supported by adolescent autonomy research and reactance theory. fileciteturn13file0L1-L1 citeturn15view2

### Secondary measures that are worth keeping

Measure **music-goal affirmation** (“This site took my goal seriously”), **cultural credibility** (“This sounded like people understood music-making rather than using rap to sell me something”), **language respect**, **perceived skill attainability**, **perceived cost**, **school-option openness**, and **non-school-option openness** separately. The perceived-cost measure is particularly important because a relevance intervention has experimentally increased utility while also increasing perceived cost. fileciteturn6file0L1-L1

Do not make “intention to attend college” the primary measure. The site is not supposed to communicate that a degree is required. A better educational-action outcome is **“identifies at least one available learning resource appropriate to the chosen goal and voluntarily begins or plans a specific next step.”**

Also avoid a single attitude item such as “School is important for my future.” It is too broad, socially loaded, and detached from behavior. The utility-value literature repeatedly points toward **specific connections between content and personally valued goals**, and behavioral evidence does not guarantee that broad attitude change follows. citeturn4search0 fileciteturn8file0L1-L1

### A defensible comparison structure

For later evaluation, the cleanest design would compare:

**Self-generated condition:** users choose a music objective and generate how a skill might help.

**Direct-message condition:** the site presents the same skill and tells the user how it helps musicians.

**Neutral process condition:** users receive factual information about the skill/process without an explicit relevance argument.

This isolates the exact theoretical distinction that matters. A second-stage study could cross self-generation with **choice vs. no choice** or **music-process framing vs. school framing**, but an initial pilot should avoid an unnecessarily complicated factorial design.

Any later randomized study must record assignment, attrition, completion, baseline music interest, baseline educational attitudes, and confidence/expectancy before the intervention. Effects should be reported overall and with predeclared moderation tests rather than only reporting whichever subgroup improves.

## Negative outcomes and falsification tests

The strongest pilot is one designed to be capable of proving the concept wrong.

| Potential harm or failure | What would reveal it | Falsification rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Reactance:** learner feels manipulated into school | Increased “hidden agenda,” pressure, anger/counterarguing, immediate exit | If intervention raises meaningful reactance versus neutral content, reject or rewrite the framing even if utility ratings rise. |
| **Rap devaluation:** site sounds like “your dream is unrealistic” | Reduced music-goal affirmation or artistic self-efficacy | Any consistent decline is a failure; the intervention is supposed to affirm ambition. |
| **School rejection gets stronger** | Post-exposure school-option openness falls relative to baseline/control | Do not claim success from higher skill awareness if educational openness worsens. |
| **Utility without action** | Relevance ratings rise but optional activity completion does not | Treat this as failure of interest-to-action transfer, consistent with the voluntary-training null RCT. fileciteturn4file0L1-L1 |
| **Perceived cost increases** | “This takes too much,” “I can't do all of this,” overwhelm measures rise | A utility gain accompanied by materially higher cost requires redesign; this pattern has appeared in a cluster RCT. fileciteturn6file0L1-L1 |
| **Rap becomes superficial bait** | Users can correctly infer that the site would say the same thing with “rap” swapped for any hobby; low authenticity ratings | Reject generic “school but with rapper nouns” content. |
| **Dialect disrespect** | Users report being corrected, stereotyped, or told their language is unprofessional | Treat as a design defect, not an acceptable trade-off. Culturally sustaining pedagogy explicitly values linguistic pluralism. fileciteturn10file0L1-L1 |
| **Famous-person testimonial dominates reasoning** | Users remember celebrity name but cannot identify a transferable skill/process | Remove or rewrite artist card; celebrity recognition is not the educational mechanism. |
| **Success-story survivorship bias** | Content implies a process guarantees industry success | Remove claim. Process stories can illustrate work practices, not establish causes of fame. |
| **Only already-engaged learners benefit** | Action effect occurs only among high-confidence/high-expectancy users | Report heterogeneity; do not generalize to the intended skeptical population. Utility effects are known to vary with expectancy. citeturn4search0 |
| **School is presented as the only legitimate route** | Users perceive postsecondary education as required despite content intent | Redesign options so formal education is explicitly one route among several. |
| **Artist examples are stale** | Low recognition/interest despite adults rating examples highly | Replace examples using measured audience response; curator preference is not evidence of teen relevance. |

A particularly important stopping rule follows from the combined literature: **an increase in “education is useful” is insufficient if voluntary action is unchanged, perceived cost rises, or autonomy declines.** Those outcomes can coexist. fileciteturn4file0L1-L1 fileciteturn6file0L1-L1

### Accessible offline formative evaluation

Before any outcome study, the static/offline prototype can be evaluated for mechanics without collecting youth data. W3C's Easy Checks support a preliminary review of page titles, headings, alternative text, contrast, text resizing, keyboard access/focus, form labeling, multimedia alternatives, motion/flashing, and basic page structure. W3C explicitly cautions that these checks are preliminary and that passing them is not equivalent to comprehensive conformance. citeturn22search6

For a locally stored prototype, evaluators can additionally conduct task-based walkthroughs without analytics: “Can someone reach a music task using only the keyboard?”, “Can all meaning survive with images/audio unavailable?”, “Does every route make the next action obvious?”, “Is anything essential dependent on hover, autoplay, high bandwidth, or an account?” These should be treated as **formative usability checks**, not evidence that the intervention changes educational behavior. ## Artist-process stories: what can and cannot responsibly be claimed

Artist examples can make invisible work visible, but they create three research risks: **survivorship bias, adult-curator bias, and causal storytelling**.

A process story should never have the structure:

> “Artist X studied/practiced/read/revised, and that is why Artist X became famous.”

That is a causal fame claim unsupported by an interview. Instead:

> “Artist X describes doing X while making this work. Here is the transferable process: revision / experimentation / collaboration / research / repeated practice. Does that process matter for anything you're working on?”

Two current women artists provide usable examples of the distinction.

A September 2026 *Marie Claire* interview with Megan Thee Stallion reports her discussing the making of the forthcoming *Act III*: she described experimenting with different vocal approaches and beats, moving outside her comfort zone, and treating the project as artistically personal. That makes a **current process example about experimentation and revision**, not evidence that experimentation caused her career success. citeturn23news38

A 2025 interview report about Doechii's writing process for “Denial Is a River” describes her delaying the writing until she had processed the experience she wanted to address and discussing vulnerability and creative development. It can illustrate **incubation, reflection, and deliberate choices about what to write**, but again cannot support the claim that those practices caused awards, fame, or commercial outcomes. No lyrics need to be reproduced. citeturn23news39

Rapsody's official site was also active in the research corpus, making her a plausible candidate for a woman-centered artist card, but I did **not** obtain a sufficiently strong first-person process source in the public material reviewed here to certify a specific process anecdote. Her card should therefore remain unshipped until a dated primary interview/source for the proposed claim is attached. citeturn22search3

This should become an editorial rule for every card:

**Process provenance:** a dated first-person interview, documentary, official behind-the-scenes account, or equivalent primary source.

**Currentness provenance:** a recent official release/tour/project/profile confirming the artist is currently active or a deliberate explanation for why a historical example is being used.

**No resonance assumption:** artist recognition, liking, and perceived relevance are empirical questions. Even a highly streamed or critically acclaimed artist cannot be assumed to resonate with a given 13–18-year-old. The interface should offer variety—including women—and eventually measure “I know this artist,” “I care about this artist,” and “this process example was useful” separately.

This also argues for **process diversity rather than celebrity density**. A useful set would cover writing/revision, beat-making/arrangement, recording, live rehearsal, collaboration, listening/research, project planning, business/rights, and experimentation. Several lesser-known or working artists may teach the process more honestly than a page composed entirely of exceptional superstars.

## Evidence gaps that should govern the project

The biggest unresolved gap is not “does relevance matter?” That has been studied extensively. The gap is the **specific transfer chain**:

> adolescent already committed to a rap goal  
> → encounters an optional website outside a required class  
> → feels ambition and linguistic identity are respected  
> → generates a personally credible relationship between music goals and learnable skills  
> → voluntarily completes a learning action  
> → later takes additional educational action  
> → does so without increased reactance, discouragement, perceived cost, or abandonment of artistic agency.

No study reviewed here tests that chain.

The second gap is **population specificity**. Qualitative hip-hop studies support the importance of authentic practice and identity affirmation, but they generally cannot tell us what proportion of contemporary U.S. 13–18-year-olds hold a “rap makes school unnecessary” belief, nor whether those who do would respond like students already participating in hip-hop programs. Foster's interviews are rich qualitative evidence about aspiration but not prevalence data. citeturn0search9 Evans's sample was self-selected/purposive and the paper itself warns about selection and instructional-context effects. citeturn20view1

The third is the **voluntary-action problem**. Evidence from required classroom assignments can overstate likely website effects because exposure and task completion have already been solved by the school environment. The high-school online-training null result is therefore at least as important to this product as the positive classroom experiments. fileciteturn4file0L1-L1

The fourth is **backfire heterogeneity**. Learners with different expectations, prior achievement, confidence, existing school experiences, and perceived costs can respond differently. Direct utility information has harmed interest/performance for low-confidence participants in experimental work, and a large adolescent classroom trial increased perceived cost even while increasing utility. fileciteturn2file0L1-L1 fileciteturn6file0L1-L1 A pilot that tests only average positive reactions could therefore miss the group it most needs to protect.

The fifth is **cultural authenticity without essentialism**. Culturally sustaining pedagogy supports respecting language and cultural practice, but that does not imply that every Black teenager likes rap, that every rap fan uses the same language varieties, or that famous artists selected by adults are meaningful to current youth. fileciteturn10file0L1-L1 The safest targeting variable is the adolescent's **expressed interest and expressed belief**, not race, neighborhood, justice involvement, or an adult's inference about what culture should appeal to them.

Finally, the evidence does **not** justify a “school saves aspiring rappers” intervention. It supports something more respectful and potentially more effective: **help young people take their artistic ambitions seriously enough to discover what they need to learn, let them make those connections themselves, and give them genuine choices about where to learn it.** Whether that produces measurable educational action is precisely the proposition the pilot should be capable of falsifying.
