01 / Writing & revision · about 5–7 minutes

Four lines. Two versions.

Make a listener picture an invented scene. Write, speak quietly, or think it through.

Original teaching example · fictional scene

The last bus pulled away.
I stood under the shelter.
The rain was loud.
I waited for a ride.

  1. Start your own scene. Use four lines about an ordinary object or fictional moment. You can also work from the example.
  2. Keep the first version. On paper, in your own notes, or in memory.
  3. Change one thing. Replace a vague detail, move a pause, or cut a word that does not help.
  4. Compare. What changed in the picture, meaning or sound?

See one possible revision

The last bus pulled away.
I folded my ticket twice.
Rain rattled the shelter roof.
I watched for familiar headlights.

This version adds visible and audible details.

If you want feedback

Ask a willing listener what they remember and where they lose the idea.

02 / Music business & arithmetic · about 5 minutes

What is left from a $500 show?

Invented scenario. Two collaborators receive a $500 fee. Travel costs $75 and an engineer costs $125. They agree to keep $100 for a future project and split the rest equally.

  1. Subtract the two expenses. How much cash remains?
  2. Set aside the agreed reserve. How much is available to split?
  3. Divide that amount between the two people.
  4. What needs to be agreed before the show? What cost might be missing?

Compare with the worked example

Fee received
$500
Travel + engineer
−$200
Cash after those expenses
$300
Agreed project reserve
−$100
Available to distribute
$200
Each person's share
$100

The reserve is cash kept for later, not automatically another expense. This does not calculate taxable profit or final after-tax income. Ask who approves costs, when payment happens and what happens if a cost changes.

03 / Delivery & meaning · about 3–5 minutes

Same words. Different emphasis.

Change what a listener notices without changing the words. Speak privately, mark the sentence on paper, or think through the meanings.

Original teaching sentence

We can start the track tomorrow.

  1. Put the emphasis on we. What might that suggest about who will start?
  2. Move it to start, then tomorrow. What changes?
  3. Try a pause after “track.” Mark it with a slash if you prefer. Does that create a different expectation?
  4. Choose an emphasis for an original line of your own. What do you want a listener to notice?

Compare three possible readings

WE might contrast this group with somebody else. START might distinguish beginning from finishing. TOMORROW might contrast tomorrow with today. Context can change the reading.

04 / Research & word choice · about 5–8 minutes

A reference that earns its place.

Source A · NASA

Our paraphrase: The Moon reflects a small part of the sunlight that reaches it.

Moonlight · NASA Science · updated February 12, 2026 · explanation of reflected light.

Source B · ESA

Our paraphrase: ESA compares planets reflecting starlight with our Moon reflecting sunlight.

What do exoplanets look like? · European Space Agency · “Clouds and surface” section · no publication date displayed; checked October 2, 2026.

  1. State the narrow fact. What do both cards support? What would go beyond them?
  2. Check where it came from. These are explanations from two agencies. Neither page attributes this explanation to the other. They are not two new experiments. Two headlines repeating one interview would still be one origin.
  3. Make two original lines. Use the idea and keep a source note. Rhyme is optional. A metaphor can be imagined; a factual claim needs support.
  4. Ask if it belongs. Does the detail help your scene or meaning, or is it there only to sound impressive?

Compare with one original example

The Moon sends borrowed sunlight down.
I carry yesterday’s idea through town.

The first line uses reflected sunlight; “borrowed” is a metaphor. The second line is imagined.

05 / Storytelling & collaboration · about 5 minutes

Same moment. Two viewpoints.

Invented rehearsal scene

Alex thinks rehearsal begins at four. Jordan thinks it begins at five. They are reading different versions of a message. Neither has checked which version is current.

  1. Write, speak privately or imagine two lines from Alex's view. What does Alex know? What is still an assumption?
  2. Try two lines from Jordan's view. Let the difference come from information, not a stereotype about either person.
  3. Choose what a listener should understand that the characters do not yet know.
  4. Write one useful question the collaborators could ask to check the plan.

Compare one possible pair

Alex: “The saved note says four; I’m ready by the door.”
Jordan: “My screen says five; I think I’m right on time.”

These original fictional lines show why each person might believe the schedule. A useful question is: “Which message is the latest, and can we confirm the time together?”

06 / Production choices & collaboration · about 5 minutes

Choose a part. Move it around.

Write, speak privately, quietly tap, or imagine the arrangement.

Four original teaching phrases

Doors open.
Lights low.
Ready now.
Take a breath.

  1. Make four parts. Invent four short phrases, patterns or descriptions of sounds. You can use our phrases instead.
  2. Choose one part. What do you want someone to notice? Repeat that part once.
  3. Change the placement. Try the repeat earlier or later, or put a pause before it. Keep two versions on paper, in your notes or in memory.
  4. Explain the difference. What changes in the meaning, pattern or feeling? Choose a version, combine them, or decide the repeat does not help.

Compare two possible arrangements

Version A: Doors open. Lights low. Ready now. Ready now. Take a breath.

Version B: Doors open. Lights low. Ready now. Take a breath. [pause] Ready now.

A could make readiness feel insistent. B could make the last phrase feel like a decision after a breath.

For feedback, ask a willing person what they notice and invite one alternative.

Badu’s account of a lesson with J Dilla

07 / Words, sounds & meanings · about 5 minutes

One word. More choices.

Write, speak quietly or think through the word choices.

Original teaching example · invented sentences

Word: light
Meaning choices: brightness; not heavy
Sound family: night, bright, out of sight

The room filled with light.
I kept my bag light.

  1. Choose one word. Try “light,” “break” or a word from your own idea. Use a dictionary if you want another meaning.
  2. Follow the meaning. Explain what your word means in this line. Could a listener understand it another way?
  3. Follow the sound. Find two words or a short phrase that repeat a sound you like. You can speak them, imagine them or mark the sounds on paper.
  4. Make and compare. Write or imagine two original lines. Change one word or phrase. Which meaning or sound helps your idea?

One way to compare choices

In the example, “light” changes meaning between the two sentences. “Bright” shares a sound with “light,” but replacing one with the other could change the grammar and meaning.

Your week

  1. 1 · Pick a small target

    Music: Choose clearer imagery, delivery, a reference or understanding a budget.

    Learning: Pick one question about an assignment, skill, credit or learning option. Name someone who could help.

  2. 2 · Collect a detail

    Music: Notice three details about an ordinary object or invented place.

    Learning: Read, listen to or discuss a short passage. Keep one question for a teacher or librarian.

  3. 3 · Check a reference

    Music: Try two lines with a checked fact, or use the provided Moon example.

    Learning: Identify what a source supports. Ask for source help if you need it.

  4. 4 · Keep two versions

    Music: Try a second version of four lines.

    Learning: Apply one revision to an assignment you choose, or ask a tutor to compare two versions with you.

  5. 5 · Explain the money

    Music: Explain the split in the invented $500 show.

    Learning: Practice the subtraction/division, or ask a math teacher or tutor where the calculation gets confusing.

  6. 6 · Try a step if it fits

    Music: Read, speak privately or silently review a short piece, or compare two arrangements.

    Learning: If useful, try one action: practice a music skill independently, use a library resource, ask a collaborator or tutor for help, begin an assignment, or ask a counselor about credits/options.

  7. 7 · Keep what helped

    Music: Compare what changed and what you want to keep.

    Learning: Name one helpful thing and one difficulty. Choose a realistic next action, change the plan, or choose none.

For adults

A flexible 20–25 minute session for ages 13–18, individually or in a small group.

Before the session

Read a story, try an activity and check the source note. Bring paper and pencils, or open the offline packet. Offer reading aloud, silent reflection and spoken responses. Do not infer reading level from age. Let the participant select a skill without choosing an artist.

Explain your setting's actual confidentiality limits.

  1. Invite · 2 minutes. “Would you like to try something connected to writing or music?” Declining is fine.
  2. Choose · 3 minutes. Let them select a story or go directly to an activity. Ask what the artist actually practiced. Then ask what the participant wants to make or understand; allow no connection.
  3. Try · 5–7 minutes. Use a fictional scene or the invented budget. No public performance; keep creative work private.
  4. Reflect · 5 minutes. “What changed? What did not help? Did this add pressure or make the goal feel harder?” Discuss the intended meaning, not whether their dialect is correct.
  5. Connect · 5 minutes. “Could a class, library, program or person help with this?” Accept that a connection may feel forced.
  6. Close · 2 minutes. Offer a voluntary next action and a reliable support route. Name who can help and how contact would happen.

If someone disagrees

“My favorite rapper dropped out.” Artists' paths vary. Black Thought's story here includes leaving college. That does not explain what caused success or choose a path for you.

“I do not need college to make music.” A college credential is not a universal requirement. What learning and support fit your goal?

“I am already good with words.” Start with that strength. Which part would you like more control over?

“School has not helped me.” What has been missing or unhelpful? A practical response may matter more than another activity.

“I need money now.” Let's find a person who can help with immediate options and a plan that fits.

“You want me to give up music.” You choose the music goal. This resource offers practice, learning choices and help.
